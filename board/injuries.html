2. The signal is a level, and what a real delta costs

Why "Out" is not a surprise. An "Out" designation is already public, and the market has priced it. A signal built on the designation itself re-states information everyone holds. The brief's requirement is the surprise — the gap between what was expected before the report and what the report then said — which needs two dated reads, not one.

The snapshot ledger dates two full-page reads of the Week-10 report, 42 hours apart, and this board computed the delta between them instead of asserting one. The trap: a naive diff of these two files is wrong, and quietly. Joined on raw keys the same two reads differ by 16 additions and 14 removals, because the older file codes the Rams as LAR and the newer one as LA, and because the practice field changed spelling between the captures (full words against abbreviations (FP/DNP/LP)), which makes it differ on all 345 shared rows while the participation fact behind it changes on only 6 of them. 28 of those 30 rows are the alias artifact: the same 14 Rams players appear on both sides with nothing changed but the code. Only the 2 Baltimore rows are real. A delta built without normalising the keys and the vocabulary would have published 28 fabricated availability events two days before kickoff, with the 2 real ones buried inside the noise. This is the case for the drift alarm in the brief: pin the parser to the expected codes and fields, and fail the build when they change, rather than publishing a delta that is really a format change.

Where they agree: the two 345-row reads (hub and audit) match on all 345 designations, zero mismatches. The two 347-row reads, taken 49 minutes apart by different modules, match on all 333 keys they share - 0 designation mismatches - while 333 of those rows differ in text, because one module writes "Full Participation in Practice" and the other writes "FP". The same page, therefore, supports either "nothing changed" or "every row changed", depending only on whether the reader knows the two encoders are the same vocabulary.

This is what the drift alarm has to be, exactly. A row-count or field-presence check would have stayed silent across all four reads, and a text-comparison check would have fired on every one of them. The alarm that works here pins three things: the expected row count for the week, the club-code set, and the practice vocabulary. A change in the last two is a parser event, not a league event, and must be reported as such rather than becoming a delta or a blank.

The practice trend is the part that matters

Of the 6 rows whose practice participation genuinely moved, 5 rose to full participation, all of them LAC, and 3 of the 6 also picked up a game designation in the same 42 hours: David Njoku (LP to FP, now Questionable), Kayode Awosika (LP to FP, now Questionable), Trey Pipkins (DNP to LP, now Questionable). That is the shape the brief is asking for: the practice field moving first and the designation following it. Read as a level, "Questionable" tells us nothing; read as the second member of a pair, it says the club moved between the two reads. It is one club in one week, so it is a pattern to test, not a feature to use, and no model on this board consumes it.

Where the moves sit, and why that is not yet a signal: 12 of the 13 designation moves belong to the two clubs whose Week-10 game was still ahead when the second read was taken — Los Angeles (Chargers) and Baltimore played Monday night, 11/16 — while the single move at a club that had already played, Arden Key's Questionable clearing to blank at Indianapolis, points the other way. A delta that only moves for clubs with a game pending is a calendar effect, not information. nfl.com/schedules/2026/by-week/week-10

Cross-checked against the tool that already does this. scripts/x3_snapshots.py is the repository's own snapshot differ and it reports the same pair as rows 345→347, designation changes 13, added 2, dropped 0 — identical to this page. Its practice figure is 3 where this page publishes 6, and the difference is definitional, not factual: the script counts a practice move only for a row whose designation did not also move (its elif at the row-comparison step), so its 3 are my 6 minus the 3 rows where both columns moved. Two correct tools, two different definitions, published side by side — which is the reason this board states the definition next to the number rather than trusting the reader to share one. scripts/x3_snapshots.py

What this does not buy us. Two reads 42 hours apart, for one week, with no third read closer to kickoff, is a delta of one step. The requirement is a series: Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, then the inactive list 90 minutes before kickoff, for every week, retained with hashes. Weeks 8 and 9 have one stored read each (data/desk/injuries.json, 320 and 362 rows) and weeks 1 to 7 have none at all, so the surprise feature cannot be backfilled - which is why this row reads PARTIAL on the register rather than done, and why no number on this board is presented as a validated personnel signal.

3. Depth of the replacement

The brief asks to track the depth of a replacement, not merely that a replacement happened, and forbids modelling depth from jersey order. The repository's replacement table holds 76 rows across the Week-10 designations. Their states:

The blank replacement cells are correct behaviour, not missing work: a club chart that does not list the injured player cannot say who replaces him. The honest output is a blank plus a reason.

The route that is verified, and the one that is not

  • Verified: official Game Books give actual starters and the inactive list for a game, with the league's own sheet as the source. That is a fact about a game that happened, not an inference about a depth chart.
  • Not verified: a league-wide snap-count source. Snap counts are the only defensible starter proxy if clubs publish nothing usable, and no official snap-count table is captured here, so the "model depth from playing time" requirement stays open.
  • Not verified: whether clubs update their depth pages after roster moves. Baltimore's quarterback depth after the Brissett trade is the live example; the chart could not be confirmed against the transaction on this pass.

4. Calibrating what "Questionable" means

Official text gives no percentage. The repository's past-season pilot joins designations to inactive lists and reports, with Wilson intervals: Questionable played 54 of 79 (68%, 95% CI 57–78); Out played 4 of 53 (7.5%, CI 3.0–17.9); Doubtful 1 of 10 (10%, CI 1.8–40.4). Those are descriptive rates on a pilot sample, not a feature, and the four "Out" players who played are published as flagged discrepancies rather than smoothed away. The n is too small to set a threshold, which is why the requirement is still open on Requirements.
